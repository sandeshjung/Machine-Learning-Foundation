What to try

  1. Move μ. The whole curve slides sideways, but its shape doesn't change.
  2. Move σ. The curve gets wider and flatter, because the total area always stays 1. The sample spreads out with it.
  3. Check the 68–95–99.7 rule. Whatever μ and σ are, about 68 % of values fall within 1σ of the mean, 95 % within 2σ and 99.7 % within 3σ. Compare the readouts.
  4. Small samples are noisy. Set n to 10 and draw a few new samples. The sample mean and standard deviation jump around their true values. With n = 5000 they barely move.
  5. Central limit theorem. In part 2, pick the skewed exponential or the two bumps, and raise n from 1 to 30. The histogram of averages becomes bell-shaped and gets narrower, exactly as σ/√n predicts.

What's going on

f(x) = 1 / (σ√(2π)) · exp(−(x − μ)² / (2σ²))

μ and σ are the parameters of the distribution. The sample mean x̄ and sample standard deviation s are estimates of them computed from data. s uses n − 1 in the denominator, which makes s² an unbiased estimate of σ².

The central limit theorem says that the average of n independent values, from almost any distribution with mean μ and standard deviation σ, is approximately normal:

x̄ ≈ N(μ, σ²/n), so the spread of the average is σ/√n
This is why mini-batch gradients get less noisy as the batch size grows: their standard deviation shrinks like 1/√B. It's also why the noise in a cross-validation score shrinks with more folds and more data.
